It Wasn't the Wall Moving, It Was the Wind

Seventh floor, south wing special exhibition zone, National Taiwan Science Education Center. A quiet floor was turned, in fourteen days, into a breathing forest: creatures in the Bouncing Valley wake beside your footsteps, spheres of light echo a rolling soundtrack, fruit on the slide scatters with the sound of children's laughter. teamLab Future Park Taipei unfolded here, and out of sight of every visitor, an entire floor's slabs, trusses, power, and light had all been recalculated, over and over.

This is a 1,785.126 square meter site, fitted out with 178 projectors, plus backend blending processors, wireless access points, and computer terminals, together weaving a sensing network that reacts to visitor behavior in real time. We handled the spatial planning, produced the construction drawings, and served as the general contractor for every on-site requirement, from day one of mobilization to teardown and clearance within seven days of closing, present through the entire run.

Hanging a Playground From a Floor You Can't Drill Into

This system demanded extreme precision. A large array of projectors had to be edge-blended with virtually no tolerance for misalignment, made harder by equipment that was already older and well used. Getting an international-standard blend out of aging hardware was, on its own, a delicate balancing act, and before any of that software could run, an entire invisible skeleton had to hold it up.

Projectors, speakers, and sensors hang from a truss below the 8th-floor slab, and the combined weight of 178 devices was never something to estimate by eye. We used custom threaded rods and steel plates to calculate and distribute the load of every device across the truss structure. Once complete, every node held steady, with no sagging or loosening anywhere in the array.

The raised access flooring underfoot carried its own challenge: in places it ran as high as 2.5 meters off the slab. At that height and span, we reinforced the structure with tightly spaced supports and additional steel, while routing the cabling through the void beneath, so load-bearing capacity and cable maintenance could coexist.

The wall was another problem. Most of the venue's walls run six meters high, and this is a public venue where none of them could be drilled or fastened into. We used a structural tie-in method instead, lashing the partitions and support structures to the existing structure behind them. When the work was inspected, the wall surfaces were untouched, exactly as they'd been before.

Curved floor-to-ceiling glass ran along two sides of the venue, and the exits, entrance, and gift shop all sat next to bright or open spaces. Interactive projection is extremely sensitive to ambient light, and any leak of outside light or reflection could throw off the projected image and confuse the sensing system.

We worked through the two curved glass walls and every entrance, exit, and shop-adjacent route one at a time, blocking light while preserving both visitor flow and fire-exit requirements, until the entire floor held as a proper blackout space.

With 178 projectors plus blending processors, access points, and terminals all running at once, power demand went far beyond what a typical exhibition space draws, so we reconfigured the electrical system from scratch. Part of the circuit runs on Hypalon cable, chosen for its resistance to abrasion, weather, and heat, and its flexibility, built to survive being routed again and again through the truss and under the raised floor as conditions shifted. Fourteen days, and all of it had to come together at once.

The Wall Never Moved

The real challenge only began after opening. The higher a system's blending precision, the less interference it can tolerate, and partway through the run, the projected image began drifting out of alignment. The initial suspicion on site fell on the wall itself. We didn't rush to a conclusion. We mounted displacement sensors on the wall, marked reference points, and had someone log readings on site every day. The data built up day by day, and the answer became clear: the wall hadn't moved.

If it wasn't the wall, the problem had to be in the projectors themselves. We added gyroscopic sensors, transmitting readings daily to track the projectors' own micro-vibration over time, narrowing in zone by zone on where the drift kept showing up. The pieces came together: the problem clustered around the summer months, during the busiest visitor periods.

The cause turned out to be almost absurdly simple. Staff, trying to keep visitors comfortable, had turned the air conditioning up to maximum. The vents had already been positioned away from the projector housings, but the stronger airflow was still enough to cause a barely perceptible vibration in the units, and that was enough to throw the blended image out of alignment.

We didn't turn the airflow down, since that would have meant trading visitor comfort for image stability. The real fix was re-wrapping and tightening the ductwork, redirecting the airflow path away from the area around each projector. Airflow stayed exactly as it was, the projectors stopped shaking, and the drift problem was resolved. What maintenance remained through the rest of the run was a separate matter, wear and tear from visitors interacting with the installations, requiring periodic servicing.

Another thread of the investigation was just as stubborn: HDMI chips began burning out in several zones. Tracing it back, we found the signal cable and power cable had all been bundled together on site, and some runs used extra-long HDMI cable to carry the signal directly over distance instead of going through a proper signal extender. The static that built up gradually damaged the chips inside the HDMI cabling.

On top of that, the computer terminals had simply been set on wire shelving with no insulation at all, adding to the static buildup. We added insulating mats under the equipment, and this kind of chip failure dropped off noticeably from there.

What the Drawings Didn't Show

Not every problem shows up on a drawing. Partway through the run, visitors began reporting a jarring impact at the bottom of the Fruit Field slide, and one visitor even produced a medical note. We assessed the situation and traced the cause to the original slide's angle, not something we could look past. We worked with the client's technical team to propose a corrected slide angle, carried out the on-site retrofit on a closed day, and adjusted the angle to soften the landing. We never heard another report of the issue afterward.

None of this troubleshooting happened behind closed doors. The suspicion about wall displacement was something our own team stayed on, reading the sensor data day after day, point by point, until it resolved. Every adjustment to floor load and power capacity had to be checked first against the venue's structural limits with the facilities team. Blocking light at the glass walls touched the fire-exit routes, which meant bringing in the fire department to sign off before we could seal anything for good.

Seven Days to Zero

When the exhibition closed, all 178 projectors, the blending processors, the access points, the computer terminals, the sensor units, the entire truss-suspension system, the raised-floor structural reinforcement, and every partition wall had to be fully dismantled and cleared within seven days, returning the site to exactly the condition it was handed over in, ready for the venue's next scheduled exhibition.
