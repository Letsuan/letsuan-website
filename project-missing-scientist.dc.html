Six Years Later, A New Story on the Same Floor

On the second floor of the Geology Science Hall at the Chelungpu Fault Preservation Park sits a science exhibit that spent six years explaining to the public why a high-voltage transmission tower isn't something to fear. By 2023 it needed to become someone new. "Electromagnetic Horizon II: The Missing Scientist" reopened on the same site, though this was never a full teardown. Across eight zones, deciding what stays and what makes way for the new story was the question the project had to answer from day one.

We took on this professional services procurement project as the party responsible for producing and updating the electromagnetic physics exhibits, exhibition design, and site construction, from mobilization through final acceptance, working through the constraints of the pandemic, to carry the entire floor from Version 1.0 into 2.0.

Keeping What's Worth Keeping

Unroll the demolition plan and only a small portion of the museum is actually circled for work. That wasn't purely a design decision. Public-sector tender budgets are finite, and a full teardown was never on the table, so we left most of the existing structure and circulation untouched. Every new design had to find its place within the existing skeleton, delivering the greatest possible transformation within a limited budget rather than clearing the floor and starting fresh.

The Tesla coil and infinity mirror wall at the entrance make the clearest case. Neither piece was broken, and tearing them out would have been wasteful, so we preserved their function as interactive teaching tools and redesigned their outer shell and narrative framing. Visitors are drawn naturally into the story of "The Missing Scientist" the moment they walk in, without realizing they're standing in front of equipment that's been there for six years.

The "Current War" light projection followed the same logic: the projection content stayed exactly as it was, and we simply repositioned Edison and Tesla, the two old rivals of scientific history, so the foundational knowledge between the old and new zones wouldn't break in half.

The row of columns in the optics zone had originally been the design's biggest headache, blocking sightlines, blocking circulation, impossible to route around. We stopped trying to route around them and turned the columns into the exhibit itself instead, redesigning the circulation around them with infrared thermal imaging: as visitors walk behind a column, the screen ahead reveals the heat signature of their own body. What was once a constraint became the one moment in the zone that makes people stop.

A Waveform You Can Finally Look Up and See

The Wave Dance Theater is the most technically demanding installation in this project. Dozens of individually motorized spheres hang from the ceiling structure, each independently raised and lowered, synchronized by program to trace the curve of transverse and longitudinal waves across a continuously shifting field of height. For the first time, visitors can look up and actually see a waveform that used to live only in a physics textbook.

Before any of that could happen, we had to answer the most basic question: could the ceiling even bear the load? We reinforced the existing ceiling structure and built a truss system to carry the suspended weight of dozens of motors and spheres, then completed positioning, wiring, and connection testing for every single point. The installation closed with an integrated AR feature that lets visitors identify the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves in real time. One installation ended up covering three disciplines at once: structural reinforcement, precision mechanical installation, and hardware-software integration.

Old Equipment Learns New Tricks

After six years, not everything in the exhibition hall was still holding up. Interactive multimedia pieces on radiation introduction, radiation shielding, and "Play with Electromagnetic Waves" had stopped functioning properly, and the measurement equipment in the household appliance testing zone had aged past reliable accuracy. Rather than replace the content outright, we updated the software and recalibrated each piece individually, so these installations, which had already guided countless families through their first encounter with electromagnetism, could keep telling the science correctly.

The LEGO Junior Makers zone got an unplanned bonus. Beyond the STEAM building activities for children, we commissioned professional LEGO builders to construct brick sculptures above the exhibit area: the Chelungpu Fault Preservation Park's mascot, the museum's signature architecture, and Nikola Tesla himself. A simple hands-on space for families gained a second life as a piece of park identity and science history on display, and became, alongside the building activities, a new spot where people stop to take photos.

The Work Nobody Sees

Some of the work never makes it onto an exhibit list but still has to get done. The entire floor was refinished in epoxy, a job we deliberately scheduled apart from the main construction window to keep dust and fumes from interfering with other trades. All the graphic panels, large-format prints, and the entrance identity were also fully updated to match the new VIS visual system, so visitors feel the shift in tone and mystery the moment they step through the door.

Running underneath the entire schedule was a challenge that never appeared on any drawing: the pandemic. Public health restrictions limited how freely we could deploy personnel and operate on site, forcing us to rework the sequencing of nearly every task. Demolition, layout, material review, partition and carpentry work, electrical routing, playback system integration testing, model installation, lighting calibration, carpet laying: all of it interlocking, all of it harder to coordinate under pandemic constraints. We still completed the full acceptance inspection within the scheduled timeframe.

12 Partners

This project was never something one team could carry alone. Specialized demolition, licensed environmental waste removal, glazing, multimedia installation, large-format print application, and metal fabrication drew on 12 partner contractors, each holding their piece of the sequence. The museum's curatorial team confirmed the exhibition content and interactive software specifications, and the design team joined us for playback system positioning tests.
