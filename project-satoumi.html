EXHIBITION — 2024NATIONAL MUSEUM OF NATURAL SCIENCE

Know the Sea by Being Close to It Central Taiwan Marine Conservation Education Center

CLIENT

Ocean Conservation Administration, Ocean Affairs Council / National Museum of Natural Science

LOCATION

Taichung, Taiwan

YEAR

2024

AREA

330㎡

CATEGORY

Exhibition Design, Interior Design, Construction

The Ocean Conservation Administration commissioned a marine conservation education center built around three Chinese white dolphins with names, turning a former restaurant inside the National Museum of Natural Science into a space that carries the story of an endangered species outward to the whole coastline it depends on. "Know the Sea by Being Close to It" is the third center under the Ocean Affairs Council's "Homage to the Sea" initiative, following earlier ones in Tainan and Penghu.

From Restaurant to Conservation Center

Opened in September 2024 on the second floor of the museum's Earth Environment Hall, the roughly 330-square-meter center combines exhibition space, offices, a science classroom, and the semi-open Dragon and Tiger Pond into a single sequence, moving visitors from viewing toward interaction, learning, and discussion. Letsuan Design took on the project as general contractor for design and construction, working from the site's existing structural conditions rather than a blank slate, and coordinating display design, mechanical and electrical work, accessibility upgrades, and construction sequencing as one continuous problem rather than separate trades.

Why the Urgency

Central Taiwan's coastline is critical habitat for the Critically Endangered Chinese white dolphin and home to several important wetlands, and its coastline, estuaries, and wetlands are inseparable from the lives of the people who live along them. Rather than starting with an animal in isolation, the exhibition starts with the dolphin and works outward — to habitat, to estuary, to wetland, and finally back to the relationship between people and the sea — so a visitor leaves understanding that the dolphin's survival is inseparable from the health of the whole coastal system.

Making the Dolphin an Ocean Messenger

The exhibition is organized around two themes — critical marine wildlife habitat and wetland conservation — under a single narrative device: the dolphin as an "ocean messenger." Visitors follow the dolphin through the waters it lives in, starting with environmental change and its life history and habitat, moving into the estuaries and wetlands of central Taiwan's coast, and arriving at the relationship between people and the ocean. The exhibition deliberately leaves its last question open — how to balance conservation against development — rather than closing it with a single answer.

A Viewpoint That Shifts With the Story

The spatial perspective was designed to track the exhibition's narrative rhythm. The entrance establishes the whole environment through a near-aerial, bird's-eye vantage point; as visitors move deeper into the space, the viewpoint shifts to eye level, then upward, then to a long view, and the scale of what's being shown narrows in step — from open ocean down to wetland, to estuary, to individual animals — building an understanding of habitat through a sequence of different spatial scales rather than a single fixed one.

Exhibits and Interaction

The entrance uses a light, clean, and layered visual language, bringing the ocean and its habitats indoors through a tiered, shadow-box style of window display. Midway through the space, multimedia displays and hands-on teaching tools translate abstract knowledge — wetlands, estuaries, marine ecology — into something visitors can operate rather than only read. At the end of the route, a multi-purpose stepped seating area and reading corner support group visits, video screenings, classes, and independent reading, alongside an interactive station where visitors can draw their own white dolphin.

Built for Learning

The space was planned around age-friendly learning, bilingual signage, assistive technology, and accessible design from the outset, so visitors of different ages, backgrounds, and needs can each find their own way into the material. The science classroom extends the exhibition content into structured programming — hands-on experiments, exploratory activities, and food-to-fish education — carrying what visitors learn in the gallery into the classroom and, from there, into daily life.

Identity and Color

The visual identity is built on clean sans-serif and rounded typefaces, refined through stroke-level detail to read as precise but approachable. The mark itself is an arched line drawn from the way light refracts through moving water and sunlight, tying together three ideas — marine ecology, forest-river-estuary, and conservation. Color runs in luminous gradients that mimic sunlight moving through water, and the icon set for each exhibit sub-theme shares the same rounded arc, so visitors can read the exhibition's structure and wayfinding at a glance, unit to unit.

Turning a Restaurant Into a Museum-Grade Space

The space had been a restaurant, and converting it to museum-grade exhibition and education use meant confronting whatever the demolition exposed — structural condition, water leaks, mechanical and electrical systems, fire safety, accessibility, and construction access — before any exhibit content could move in.

A Floor-Level Gap and a Leak

Once the former restaurant was demolished down to the structural slab, the interior floor sat more than 7cm below the level of the exterior corridor, and part of the slab had been damaged during demolition. Once work began, the team also found water leaking through what looked like an expansion joint — tracing it back, the actual source turned out to be a drain head outside a planter bed, not the joint itself. The general contractor corrected the floor-level gap, and the leak was repaired at its actual source, giving the interior fit-out a stable base to build from.

An HVAC Room: Fire Safety and Noise at Once

The office area sits directly next to the central air-conditioning plant room, which meant the layout had to account for both the noise the equipment generates and the fire code it triggers. The team added rock wool as fire-rated material and additional sound insulation on the plant-room side, cutting the vibration and noise reaching the office enough to keep it usable as a workspace.

An Accessibility Review That Touched the Whole Museum

Once the project filed for a change-of-use permit and interior renovation review, accessibility became a building-wide question rather than a room-by-room one. The men's restroom was reconfigured — entrance relocated, undersized sinks and urinals removed, fixture counts and spacing corrected, a child-height urinal and accessibility grab bars added. The accessible restroom's existing opening didn't meet code, so the door frame and wall had to be re-cut and rebuilt; that work was scheduled on the museum's closed days, using heavy equipment to cut out the old frame and wall so the demolition and rebuild never touched the museum's normal visiting hours. The review extended to accessible parking, the elevator, and floor signage; the existing stairwell's riser height and tread depth couldn't be brought to current code without compromising the existing structure, so a wheelchair lift was installed instead to resolve vertical access.

Reconnecting What Demolition Had Cut

Early demolition had cut wall outlets and sections of wiring, leaving the existing circuits unusable, so the entire electrical system had to be rerun. A new pantry area in the office needed its own water supply and drainage; the science classroom's food-to-fish education program needed a dedicated circuit for induction cooktops and matching plumbing.

Moving Materials Without Closing the Museum

The museum had to stay open throughout construction, and routing the cement and mortar needed to rebuild the floor through the public circulation route would have put it directly in visitors' path. Instead, the team removed a window on the upper floor and used a winch to lift materials in from outside, keeping the museum's existing gallery circulation completely clear of construction traffic.

Five Exhibit Units

01 — Forest-River-Sea and Estuary Ecology. Starting from the idea of "satoumi" and the Satoumi Initiative, this unit explains how central and western Taiwan's coast is at once a place people live and critical habitat for the Chinese white dolphin and the finless porpoise, presenting a picture of people and nature living alongside each other.

02 — Taiwan Cetacean Intelligence Wall. Built from three sections — a whale-and-dolphin sighting map, a seasonal white dolphin observation report, and a finless porpoise stranding report — updated each season with current information on cetaceans in Taiwan's waters.

03 — Wetland Ecology. Models present the landscape of central Taiwan's key wetlands alongside stranded-dolphin specimens, wetland crab specimens, and specimens of shorebirds and raptors, showing the wetland as a habitat that supports many different forms of life at once.

04 — Dolphins of Critical Habitat. A large tabletop display carries the life-history knowledge of the white dolphin and the finless porpoise, with a 1:1 dolphin model and skeletal specimens suspended above it as the center's signature exhibit unit.

05 — Dragon and Tiger Pond: Three Named Dolphins. A water curtain rises above the Dragon and Tiger Pond, with three dolphin models named after their real Ocean Conservation Administration ID numbers — OCA001 "Camellia," OCA004 "Fountain," and OCA008 "Strawberry Milk" — as the physical embodiment of the center's "ocean messenger" narrative.

Design Summary

The project spans converting a former restaurant, exhibition narrative, visual identity, interactive education, accessibility, mechanical and electrical work, and construction, all coordinated at once so a marine conservation education center could actually open and run. Letsuan Design worked from the integration of space and exhibition outward, turning the relationship between the Chinese white dolphin, wetlands, estuaries, and the people who live alongside them into something visitors can see, read, operate, and experience.
