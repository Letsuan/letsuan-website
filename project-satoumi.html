Knowing the Sea Central Taiwan Marine Conservation Education Center

CLIENT

Ocean Conservation Administration, Ocean Affairs Council / National Museum of Natural Science

LOCATION

National Museum of Natural Science, Taichung, Taiwan

YEAR

2024

AREA

Approx. 330㎡ (~100 ping) interior + semi-open pool area

CATEGORY

Exhibition Design, Interior Design, Construction

Knowing the Sea, the Central Taiwan Marine Conservation Education Center, is built around the story of the Taiwanese white dolphin and the coastal ecology of central Taiwan. The project converted an existing restaurant space into a marine conservation education center that combines exhibition, education, office, and public program functions.

Letsuan Design served as design-build contractor, integrating spatial planning, exhibition design and fabrication, a science classroom, accessibility improvements, and MEP work, so the knowledge the exhibition conveys extends into the space itself.

Overview

The Central Taiwan Marine Conservation Education Center opened in September 2024 on the second floor of the Earth Environment Gallery at the National Museum of Natural Science, commissioned by the Ocean Conservation Administration.

The project centers on exhibition and education, integrating an education center, offices, exhibition areas, a science classroom, and the semi-open Dragon and Tiger Pond. Spatial planning at different scales lets visitors move from viewing the exhibits into interaction, learning, and discussion.

Letsuan Design worked from the existing building conditions, reorganizing the circulation and functional layout while coordinating exhibition, MEP, accessibility, and construction requirements to carry the design intent through to the completed space.

Context

This is the third marine conservation education center under the Ocean Conservation Administration's four-year habitat conservation program, following centers in Tainan and Penghu.

The coast of central Taiwan is critical habitat for the endangered Taiwanese white dolphin and includes several important wetlands. These coasts, estuaries, and wetlands are interconnected and closely tied to the lives of the people who live along them. The exhibition therefore begins with the white dolphin and widens outward to its habitat, estuaries, and wetlands before returning to the relationship between people and the sea, helping visitors see how the dolphin's survival is tied to the health of the wider marine environment.

Letting the White Dolphin Carry the Story

The exhibition is built around two themes, critical marine wildlife habitat and wetland conservation, narrated through a "Messenger of the Sea" storyline. Visitors follow a white dolphin through the waters where it lives: starting with environmental change, moving into the dolphin's life history and habitat, then into the estuaries and wetlands of the central Taiwan coast, and finally back to the relationship between people and the sea. Rather than offering a single answer, the exhibition closes with space to consider how conservation and development might be balanced.

Perspective

The spatial perspective follows the pacing of the exhibition narrative. The entrance establishes the broader environment through a near aerial viewpoint, which shifts as visitors move deeper into the space toward eye level, upward, and distant views. The scale of observation narrows in step, from the open sea down to wetlands, estuaries, and individual animals, building an understanding of habitat through spatial experience at different scales.

Exhibition Devices and Interactive Installations

The entrance establishes a first impression through a light, clean, layered visual language, using stepped display windows to bring the imagery of the sea and its habitats indoors. The midsection of the exhibition combines multimedia displays with interactive teaching tools, translating more abstract knowledge about wetlands, estuaries, and marine ecology into content visitors can operate and understand. At the end of the circulation route, a multi-use stepped activity area and reading corner support group visits, film screenings, programs, and independent reading, with an interactive station where visitors can draw their own white dolphin.

Learning

The spatial plan also accounts for age-friendly learning, bilingual signage, assistive technology, and accessible design, so visitors of different ages, backgrounds, and needs can find a way to read and learn that works for them. The science classroom extends the exhibition content into hands-on coursework, using experiments, exploratory activities, and a seafood-to-table education program to connect what visitors learn in the exhibition to classroom study of the marine environment and daily life.

Identity and Color System

The visual identity is built on sans-serif and rounded typefaces, with variations in stroke detail that give it a clean, approachable, neutral character. The logo uses an arched line as its core symbol, drawn from the movement of the sea and the way sunlight refracts through water, linking the three themes of marine ecology, forest-river-estuary systems, and conservation.

Color is built mainly from light-filled gradients that echo how natural light shifts as it passes through water, drawing on a core palette of ocean (#0F3D44), current (#2F8F8A), wetland (#7FB9A8), estuary (#D9CDB3), and light (#C98F4A). The icons for each sub-theme throughout the exhibition share the same rounded forms, keeping the different display units visually consistent and helping visitors read the themes and wayfinding.

Engineering Challenges

This space was originally a restaurant. Converting it into an education and exhibition space that met museum requirements meant the construction team first had to resolve the structural, waterproofing, MEP, fire safety, accessibility, and material logistics issues left behind after demolition, the work that had to happen before the exhibition content could move in.

Floor Leveling and Leak Repair

After the existing restaurant was demolished, the floor had been cut down to the structural slab, leaving a step of more than 7 centimeters between the interior and the exterior corridor, with parts of the floor slab damaged during demolition. On entering the site, the team also found a leak at the building's expansion joint. After repeated investigation, the source turned out not to be the expansion joint itself but a drain connection outside a planter box. The floor was rebuilt and leveled by the general contractor, and the leak was repaired at its actual source, giving the interior work a stable base to build on.

HVAC Room: Fire Safety and Noise

The planned office sits next to the central HVAC room, so beyond the layout itself, the project also had to address the noise and fire safety conditions created by the equipment. The partition uses calcium silicate board together with 60K-density rock wool and low-formaldehyde board to reach a one-hour fire rating, with additional sound insulation added on the mechanical room side, reducing the vibration and noise transmitted into the office to a workable level.

A Facility-Wide Accessibility Review

Applying for a change to the building usage license and submitting the interior renovation for review triggered a full accessibility review of the entire space.

The existing men's restroom was reconfigured, relocating the entry door, removing sinks and urinals that did not meet code, and resetting the fixture count and spacing to requirement, adding a child-height urinal and grab bars throughout. The accessible restroom's existing doorway fell short of code, which requires at least 80 centimeters of clear width once the door is open; because this is a science museum, the government project office required 90 centimeters instead. The door frame and wall were rebuilt during museum closing days, using heavy equipment to cut and rebuild the opening to that standard without affecting visitor circulation while the museum was open.

The review also extended to accessible parking, elevators, and floor signage. The existing straight staircase did not meet accessibility standards in riser height or tread depth, but because it involved the building's structural safety, it could not be rebuilt directly, so a wheelchair lift was installed instead to resolve vertical circulation.

Rewiring After Demolition

The earlier demolition had cut wall outlets and sections of wiring flush, leaving the existing circuits unable to be reconnected, so the entire area's electrical system had to be rewired. The office gained a new pantry area with matching water supply and drainage; the science classroom, built around a seafood-to-table curriculum, needed a dedicated circuit for an induction cooktop along with matching water supply and drainage.

Material Logistics That Kept the Museum Open

The museum needed to stay open throughout construction, so the cement mortar required to rebuild the floor could not be carried through the public circulation route without affecting visitors. The team instead removed a glass window on the floor above and used a winch to lift material directly from outside the building to the work floor, keeping the museum's existing gallery circulation completely clear of construction.

Five Exhibition Zones

01 — Forest, River, Satoumi, and Estuary Ecosystems. Drawing on the concept of satoumi and the Satoumi Initiative, this zone explains how the west coast and shoreline of central Taiwan serve as both a place where people live and critical habitat for the Taiwanese white dolphin and the finless porpoise, presenting a picture of people and nature coexisting.

02 — Taiwan Cetacean Information Wall. Made up of three sections, a sighting map, a seasonal white dolphin survey report, and a finless porpoise stranding report, updated quarterly with information on cetaceans around Taiwan's waters.

03 — Wetland Ecology. Models recreate the landscape of central Taiwan's major wetlands, alongside specimens of stranded dolphins, wetland crabs, and shorebirds and raptors, showing wetlands as habitat for a wide range of species.

04 — Dolphins of a Critical Habitat. A large tabletop display presents the life histories of the white dolphin and the finless porpoise, with 1:1 scale dolphin models and skeletal specimens suspended above, the center's key display unit.

05 — Dragon and Tiger Pond: Three Dolphins with Names. A water curtain rises over Dragon and Tiger Pond, above three white dolphin models named after their Ocean Conservation Administration tracking numbers: OCA001 "Camellia," OCA004 "Fountain," and OCA008 "Strawberry Milk," a physical expression of the center's "Messenger of the Sea" narrative.

Closing

The project spans the conversion of an existing restaurant, exhibition narrative, visual identity, interactive education, accessibility, MEP, and construction, all of which had to come together for the marine conservation education center to function. Letsuan Design worked from the integration of space and exhibition, turning the relationships among the white dolphin, wetlands, estuaries, and human life into a space that can be seen, read, operated, and experienced.
