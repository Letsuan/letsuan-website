Curatorial Concept: A Package Waiting to Be Opened

A sealed box gives away nothing about what it holds until someone unwraps it, lays it flat, and lets it spread open panel by panel into what packaging designers call a die-line.

The history of the Ministry of Culture works the same way. It runs from the founding of the Council for Cultural Affairs in the 1980s, through the Government Information Office's role in media and communications, to the Ministry's own establishment in 2012, and each of those organizational restructurings reads like a fold line pressed into the packaging.

What the Ministry does today, across its various bureaus and divisions, is the content that only becomes visible once the box is unfolded. The exhibition takes "Unfold, See the Ministry of Culture" as its curatorial theme, using the packaging die-line as both visual language and spatial framework: visitors move through the space the way they'd unwrap a gift by hand, watching each fold line of the Ministry's history flatten out and become legible until they arrive at a clear picture of what the Ministry actually does.

The Unfolding Diagram as Narrative Structure

In packaging design, the unfolding diagram is the flat pattern a three-dimensional box produces when it's opened out. Its panels, the lid, the side flaps, the base, the corner tabs, are connected but distinct: folded, they form a complete box; flattened, they read like separate pages.

That structural logic became the exhibition's narrative framework on two levels at once. Each fold line in the diagram lines up with a milestone in the Ministry's history (the founding of the Council for Cultural Affairs, the absorption of the Government Information Office's functions, the Ministry's inauguration), so crossing a fold line means completing a chapter of the story. Each panel, meanwhile, lines up with one of the Ministry's current bureaus or affiliated agencies, so visitors leave understanding both where the Ministry came from and what it oversees now, without needing two separate systems to explain it.

Circulation: From Sealed Box to Unfolded Diagram

The circulation follows the psychological experience of unwrapping a package, in three stages. The entrance is a monumental sealed packing box showing only the Ministry's emblem and minimal text, building curiosity before the unwrapping even starts.

Past the entrance, the space opens into an actual unfolding-diagram floor and wall layout, so visitors feel like they're walking across a flattened box. Each fold line they cross carries them into the next historical period; each panel functions as its own zone introducing one division. Mapped against the Ministry's current structure: the lid panel belongs to the Department of Planning, which sets cultural policy direction, mid- and long-term plans, and cross-sector coordination, and sits at the "lid" as the overview entry point before the six functional panels.

The six side-flap panels cover the Ministry's six operational bureaus: the Department of Cultural Resources (heritage, museums, community development, cultural facilities), the Department of Cultural Industries (creative-industry development, cross-strait economic exchange, technology), the Department of Motion Pictures and Popular Music Industries (film, radio, television, popular music policy), the Department of Humanities and Publications (literary development, publishing, government publications), the Department of Arts (visual arts, performing arts, everyday aesthetics), and the Department of Cultural Exchange (international and cross-strait cultural cooperation).

The base panel closes with a summary timeline of the Ministry's history, looping back up to the Department of Planning at the lid, so the spatial logic runs from historical context to policy direction and back again. At the exit, echoing the sealed box at the entrance, an interactive installation invites visitors to "re-seal" the package by choosing the Ministry function that matters most to them and marking it on a digital box surface, leaving behind their own hope for what comes next.

Visual and Graphic Design

The visual identity system runs on fold lines, cut lines, and registration marks (the crosshair symbols used in print production), appearing consistently across the wayfinding system, panel borders, and timeline nodes.

The color scheme builds on the Ministry's brand colors, with each panel assigned its own secondary accent by content theme, much like color blocks separate product lines in packaging design, so visitors know at a glance which division they're standing in.

Each panel is kept to what can be read at a glance, so the diagram's clean visual character isn't buried under dense text; anything deeper is handed off to the interactive touch points.

Exhibit and Interactive Design

Floor and wall lighting shifts as visitors cross each fold line, reinforcing the sense of time advancing and structure unfolding. The departmental multimedia stations are the core of the interactive design, built to answer one specific need: letting the public see what each department actually does and where national cultural policy currently stands.

Each of the six functional panels carries a touchscreen station with three layers of content. The first layer is an at-a-glance infographic of the department's core responsibilities. The second layer tracks what's happening now: current flagship policies and project status (the Department of Cultural Resources' heritage restoration work, the Department of Cultural Industries' support programs, the Department of Motion Pictures and Popular Music Industries' grants and incentives, and so on), supplied by each department and refreshed on a regular cycle so the content stays current. The third layer points forward, linking to the Ministry's official website or open-data resources so visitors who want to can keep following policy after they leave.

All six stations share one interface language, layout, department color coding, and iconography, so visitors can identify what they're looking at even browsing out of order. The lid-panel station for the Department of Planning uses dynamic data visualization to show how the six departments' work coordinates, giving visitors an overview before they go deeper, and doubles as the navigational index for the six panels.

At the exit, the interactive re-sealing wall collects visitors' hopes and feedback on cultural policy, closing the narrative loop back to the sealed box at the entrance while giving the Ministry a channel for public input.

Structure, Materials, and Lighting

The building's occupancy permit calculated the entire ground floor as a single area, which meant anything fixed to the structure would trigger a formal interior-alteration review. To leave that permit status untouched, the whole exhibition was designed as non-fixed construction: every plinth, panel, and volume stands free, anchored to neither the slab nor the beams and columns.

The trade-off was that center of gravity, ballasting, and overturning risk had to be verified element by element to keep the gallery safe under crowd loading and seismic activity, and this ended up being the most time-consuming technical problem on the project.

The primary structure uses clean white and light-gray volumes, in keeping with the durable, no-nonsense character expected of a government exhibition space, while select panels bring in corrugated-cardboard texture or matte finishes as a material echo of packaging, kept just short of tipping into a commercial look. The main circulation runs on even ambient lighting; fold lines and panel edges are traced with linear light strips to reinforce the diagram's structural lines; and key installations, like the emblem-reveal device, get focused spot lighting for a sense of ceremony.

Closing

The unfolding diagram isn't a visual gimmick so much as a narrative tool matched to what this exhibition needed to do: turning organizational history, the kind of material that usually ends up as a bullet list or an official document, into something visitors unwrap by hand and understand step by step. What visitors carry out isn't only the history from the Council for Cultural Affairs to the present Ministry, but a sense that what the Ministry actually does sits closer to daily life than they expected.
